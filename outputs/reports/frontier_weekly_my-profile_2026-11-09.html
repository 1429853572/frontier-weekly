Genetic Background and Metabolic Influence on Long-Term Lithium Bioaccumulation in Mytilus Mussels and Their Hybrids Core
Environmental Science & Technology · 2026-11-06 · Hyeryeong Jeong; Daniel Ferreira Araújo; Rossana Sussarellu; Arbia Jouini; Laurine Payant et al.
Abstract Marine mussels are widely used as sentinel organisms in coastal biomonitoring, yet the biological mechanisms controlling the bioaccumulation of emerging metals remain poorly constrained. Climate change and anthropogenic disturbances are reshaping species distributions and contact zones, promoting mussel hybridization that may alter physiological traits and contaminant-handling capacities. Lithium (Li) remains largely overlooked despite its potential toxicity and rapidly increasing global demand. Here, we investigate the influence of critical factors on Li bioaccumulation by mussels combining genetic background and isotopic approaches. Progenies of two Mytilus species and their hybrids were transplanted to the same Atlantic coastal site for 12 months. Over this in situ exposure period, Li concentrations show comparable temporal trends across all progeny groups, with an initial uptake-dominated phase followed by biodilution. Lithium isotope ratios (δ7Li) display a coherent temporal trend across all groups (decrease at early exposure, increase during later growth), and the magnitude of the shift differs slightly among genetic backgrounds. Across samples, δ7Li is coupled to growth-related metrics (dry weight) and inversely related to Li concentrations, indicating a metabolic control on Li isotope compositions of these organisms. Li concentrations are also positively correlated with lithogenic-associated elements (e.g., aluminum), consistent with the influence of particle associated Li. A simplified biodynamic modeling including Li isotopes data further indicates a more efficient Li depuration after 5 months, combined with a relatively stronger contribution from dissolved Li. Overall, growth-related factors emerged as the primary biological drivers of Li concentration and isotope variability, with the relative contributions of dissolved and particulate exposure pathways varying in association with physiological growth. Hybridization may modulate Li bioaccumulation and δ7Li values, but its influence appears secondary and does not compromise the use of Mytilus mussels as bioindicators in established frameworks such as “Mussel Watch”.
Progressive shifts in soil respiration response to long-term nitrogen enrichment across global forests and grasslands Core
Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences · 2026-11-04 · Aijun Xing; Mengying Zhao; Shangshi Liu; Arthur A. D. Broadbent; Enzai Du et al.
Soil stores the largest pool of actively cycling carbon (C) on land, and its dynamics under global changes are critical for projecting climate feedbacks. Global nitrogen (N) deposition has more than doubled since the 1860s; however, the temporal dynamics of soil C emissions under prolonged N deposition are poorly understood. Here, using a global time-series dataset compiled from 38 long-term experiments in forests and grasslands, we find that the response of soil respiration (Rs) to N addition systematically shifts from a transient increase persisting for approximately 2 y to a sustained suppression over time (mean effect = −6%; 95% CI = [−10%, −3%]). Rs in forests and grasslands followed consistent nonlinear trajectories, characterized by a rapid initial decline in the magnitude of the response, which progressively leveled off. Notably, suppression of Rs emerged when cumulative N inputs exceeded a threshold of ~100 kg N ha −1 . Consequently, the suppression was both stronger and occurred more rapidly under high-level N additions (>50 kg N ha −1 y −1 ). These temporal dynamics were associated with N-induced soil acidification and related changes in microbial properties. Overall, these findings indicate that accurate prediction of land–atmosphere C fluxes under global change requires models to capture the reported temporal dynamics.
Coordinated soil–plant–microbial responses shaped by moderate grazing intensity in subalpine mountain grasslands Core
Agriculture Ecosystems & Environment · 2026-11-04 · S. Quintana-Esteras; Pilar Santolaria; C. Martí
Mountain grasslands of the Central Pyrenees are highly sensitive socio-ecological systems traditionally managed under extensive grazing, where soil properties, vegetation dynamics and microbial processes are tightly coupled. However, the effects of grazing are often assessed separately for either soil or forage, which constrains integrated evaluations of overall ecosystem functioning. In this study, we compared two subalpine grassland plots subjected to contrasting grazing intensities (moderate-high vs. moderate-low), integrating soil physical, chemical and biological indicators with forage nutritional quality. Grazing intensity was characterized using GPS-derived cattle movement data from five adult animals during the grazing season. Soils under lower grazing intensity retained more water, accumulated larger organic carbon pools and exhibited longer carbon residence times together with higher microbial functional diversity. By contrast, higher grazing intensity favored labile carbon dynamics, with more dissolved organic carbon and higher microbial respiration. No significant differences were observed in mesofauna diversity, total glomalin content or β-glucosidase activity. Although forage composition was dominated by mixed grasses and legumes, nutritional quality was low across all plots, due to high fiber content and deficiencies in Zn, Cu and Se. Multivariate analyses showed that soil carbon and hydrological status drive ecosystem variability through coordinated soil–microbial–forage responses. Our results suggest that even subtle variations in grazing intensity generate integrated functional shifts across ecosystem compartments. Under ongoing climate change, such tightly coupled systems may be particularly vulnerable to grazing mismanagement, emphasizing the need for adaptive strategies that integrate soil health, microbial functioning and forage quality to sustain mountain grassland resilience.

Radioactive fallout legacy of nuclear tests in soils of the Southern Hemisphere Proxy
Communications Earth & Environment · 2026-11-07 · Aydoğan Avcıoğlu; Gerald P. Dicen; Surya Gupta; Christine Alewell; Olivier Evrard et al.
Abstract Fallout radionuclide (FRN) archived in soils are key tracers of soil redistribution, sediment dynamics, and associated carbon fluxes, yet their global application is limited by poorly constrained baselines in the Southern Hemisphere. Here, we present the first hemisphere-scale baseline and uncertainty maps of 137 Cs and 239+240 Pu soil inventories. Predictions exhibited lower spatial variability and relative uncertaintyfor 137 Cs across most landscapes, whereas 239+240 Pu showed greater spatial variability and larger relative uncertainty. The reconstructed inventories revealed strong latitudinal and topographic gradients, with the highest predicted fallout occurring in tropical and mountainous regions. We further show, under a standardized hypothetical 50% inventory-loss scenario, that uncertainty in low-inventory regions can propagate nonlinearly into soil erosion and carbon-budget estimates, producing carbon budget biases of up to 19.8 t C ha −1 . These findings provide a new benchmark for Southern Hemisphere fallout radionuclide applications and a scalable framework for integrating spatial uncertainty into sediment tracing, soil degradation assessment, and carbon cycle studies.
Occurrence and Environmental Weathering of Agricultural Microplastics on Beaches across Japan: Insights into Surface Mineralization and Metal Binding Proxy
Environmental Science & Technology · 2026-11-07 · Naoya Katsumi; Misako Kawasaki; Takasei Kusube; Yutaro Takaya; Yohey Hashimoto
Abstract We investigated the coastal occurrence and environmental weathering of microplastics derived from polymer-coated fertilizers (PCFs) across Japan. A field survey along the Japanese coastline revealed that PCF-derived microplastics are widely distributed on beaches throughout the country, indicating that agricultural microplastics have dispersed far beyond agricultural landscapes. Compositional analysis showed that these particles consist predominantly of polyolefins (∼70%) and polyurethanes (∼30%) and commonly exhibit oxidative degradation accompanied by iron enrichment. Bulk elemental analysis demonstrated positive correlations between iron and multiple heavy metals. Micro-X-ray fluorescence mapping using a focused 20-μm beam showed that chromium is heterogeneously distributed on the plastic surface and spatially associated with iron, while X-ray absorption spectroscopy indicated its occurrence in ferrihydrite-like phases or as chromite. Although detailed spectroscopic evidence was obtained from a single selected particle, the widespread occurrence of PCF-derived microplastics and their observed metal enrichment suggest that similar geochemical processes may occur at other coastal sites. By linking nationwide field observations with process-level characterization, this study shows that microplastics have the potential to transition from inert debris to geochemically reactive substrates after coastal deposition.
Elevated early Eocene atmospheric CO2 tied to reduced Southern Ocean biological pump efficiency Proxy
Nature Geoscience · 2026-11-06 · Simone Moretti; François Fripiat; Mathis P. Hain; Valeria Luciani; Lucas Joost Lourens et al.
Abstract The Early Eocene Climatic Optimum (EECO) was an interval in Earth’s history when reconstructed temperatures and atmospheric CO2 concentrations were among the highest of the Cenozoic Era (i.e., the last 66 million years). The causes of the high CO2 levels remain unclear. The Southern Ocean, a major “leak” in the global ocean’s biological carbon pump that has been implicated in the CO2 changes over recent ice age cycles, may also have played a role earlier in the Cenozoic. Here, we report foraminifera-bound δ15N measurements that show higher surface nitrate concentrations in the Southern Ocean during peak EECO. The associated venting of CO2 from the deep ocean to the atmosphere would have been amplified by the low seawater pH buffering capacity of the Eocene. Previously proposed low silicate rock weatherability during the Eocene may have weakened the “weathering thermostat”, allowing the reconstructed change in biological pump efficiency in the Southern Ocean to explain at least half of the atmospheric CO2 increase observed during the EECO.

Environmental Science & Technology · 2026-11-06 · Mengqi Zhang; Yawen Tian; Yitao Pan; Zhiwen Cheng; Jiayin Dai et al.
Abstract Hexafluoropropylene oxide (HFPO)-based compounds have been increasingly adopted as replacements for legacy per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) such as perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA), yet evidence on developmental toxicity remains limited. This study investigated structure-dependent maternal-fetal transfer, fetal tissue distribution, and hepatic effects of PFOA and four HFPO-based alternatives, including hexafluoropropylene oxide dimer acid (HFPO-DA) and hexafluoropropylene oxide trimer acid (HFPO-TA) homologues, in gestational mice. All compounds were detected in maternal serum, fetal tissues, and amniotic fluid in a dose-dependent manner, but transplacental transfer efficiencies varied markedly. These findings reveal structure-dependent fetal liver accumulation and support incorporating fetal-specific endpoints into PFAS risk assessment.
Mechanisms sustaining stable crop yields under long-term drip irrigation in arid regions Proxy
npj Sustainable Agriculture · 2026-11-05 · Chen Yaning; Zhenhua Wang; Feihu Yin; Chenggang Zhu; Chuanxiu Liu
Drip irrigation has been widely practiced in the arid zones of Northwest China for three decades, yet its long-term impacts on soil salinization dynamics remain hotly debated. While drip irrigation effectively desalinates crop root zones, it simultaneously induces salt accumulation at wetting fronts, in inter-film soils, and between crop rows, posing a potential risk of secondary salinization. The coupling of drip irrigation with autumn-winter irrigation achieves off-season salt leaching, thereby sustaining long-term soil health and stabilizing crop yield. Under the constraint of limited reservoir water storage capacity, this integrated system enables the reuse of otherwise idle winter water to maintain a stable soil water-salt balance, providing valuable insights for the sustainable development of agriculture in arid regions across the globe.
Crop succession mitigates cumulative soil compaction and preserves soil physical quality under silage maize production Proxy
Soil and Tillage Research · 2026-11-05 · Betsy Carolina Muñoz de Páez; Eduardo da Costa Severiano; André Ribeiro Silveira; Teotônio Soares de Carvalho; Júlio Sílvio de Sousa Bueno Filho et al.
Brazilian silage maize production relies on intensive machinery traffic, which may progressively impair soil physical quality under continuous monoculture. This study evaluated whether soybean succession mitigates cumulative soil compaction and preserves soil physical quality in tropical Oxisols managed under silage maize over two consecutive crop seasons. Despite the widespread adoption of silage maize in tropical regions, little is known about how crop succession modifies the temporal evolution of soil compaction and soil physical quality under repeated machinery traffic in these systems. A quasi-experimental time-series design was adopted. Soil samples were collected from the 0–5 and 20–25 cm layers on four sampling occasions. Initial samples were used to develop load-bearing capacity models (LBCM) and identify the initial soil properties, followed by three successive samplings before and after harvest in each crop cycle. Bulk density (Bd), pre-consolidation pressure (σp), total porosity (Tp), microporosity (Mic), macroporosity (Mac), and organic matter (OM) were determined. LBCMs were developed and fitted to a regression model of the type σp = 10(a + bθ). Other soil properties were analyzed using linear mixed models. Crop succession reduced the proportion of compacted samples throughout the study and improved key indicators of soil physical quality, including Bd and Mic. In contrast, continuous silage maize monoculture promoted progressive subsurface compaction, increased microporosity, and reduced Mac. The σp proved more sensitive than Bd for detecting management-induced changes in soil mechanical behavior. Overall, soybean succession mitigated the cumulative effects of repeated machinery traffic, preserving soil physical quality under continuous silage maize production. These findings highlight load-bearing capacity models as valuable tools for monitoring soil compaction and supporting sustainable soil management in tropical production systems.
Global Potential of No-Tillage in Enhancing Soil Carbon and Reducing Greenhouse Gas Emissions While Sustaining Crop Yields Proxy
Environmental Science & Technology · 2026-11-05 · Chuanxu Fang; Xiaorong Wei; Wim DE VRIES; Gerard H. Ros; Josep Peñuelas et al.
Abstract Pursuing the balance between environmental sustainability and agricultural productivity remains a critical challenge for achieving global sustainable development goals. No-tillage (NT), as a key measure of sustainable conservation and climate-smart agriculture, offers a promising approach to addressing this challenge. However, its comprehensive effects on soil carbon accumulation, greenhouse gas emissions, and crop yields remain unquantified at the global scale, limiting our ability to optimize both environmental and agronomic outcomes. Here, we conducted such a global synthesis by combining a meta-analysis of 8,303 paired observations from 1,371 studies with a random forest model to assess the influence of site conditions and management on NT effects. Our meta-analysis demonstrates integrated benefits of NT in enhancing soil organic carbon (SOC) stocks in 0–30 cm layer by 11.0% and mitigating greenhouse gas (the sum of CO2, CH4, and N2O in CO2-equivalent) emissions by 13.5%, without a significant impact on crop yields (−0.6% ± 1.5%). The integrated benefits are stronger in drier climates and coarser-textured soils, and they exhibit significant time-dependent patterns. Our prediction identifies 44% of global croplands as being optimal for NT’s integrated benefits, mainly in Africa, South Asia, and North America. Adopting NT in these areas would result in a 4.8% increase in major food-crop yields, a 15.2% increase in 0–30 cm SOC stocks, and a 6.4% reduction in global cropland emissions, respectively. These findings underscore the relevance of implementing NT in suitable regions by adopting it into Good Agricultural Practice Guidelines.
The cost of capital is the main barrier to Africa’s energy transition Proxy
Communications Sustainability · 2026-11-04 · Rabah Arezki
Africa contributes less than four percent of global greenhouse-gas emissions yet faces some of the gravest consequences of climate change. Clean technology is no longer the obstacle to its energy transition: solar power is the cheapest source of new electricity in much of the world, and Africa holds the finest solar resource on the planet. In this Perspective, we argue that the binding constraint is instead the cost of capital. Because renewable generation is capital-intensive and front-loaded, its cost is acutely sensitive to financing terms, and identical projects cost far more in Africa than in Europe. The risk premium is the price at which weak utilities, inconvertible currencies, shallow markets and fragile public finances are charged back to every project. We examine the mechanisms that can lower or absorb that premium. The objective is not more capital, but cheaper and more patient capital. Renewable technologies are now affordable, but clean-energy deployment in Africa remains constrained by expensive financing. Because renewable projects require large upfront investments, lowering the cost of capital could be the most effective way to accelerate the energy transition.

Mountain grassland SOC stocks decline for 30 years with low N mineral fertilisation, independent of yield size and temperature increase Proxy
Agriculture Ecosystems & Environment · 2026-11-03 · Matthias Volk; Elisa R.R. Filippini; Sonja G. Keel
The carbon (C) stocks of grassland soils represent one of the largest potential sinks for the greenhouse gas (GHG) CO 2 . This makes knowledge of the changes in the soil organic C (SOC) stock essential. In a long-term experiment, we studied mountain grassland yields and topsoil SOC stocks in a one-level N and six-level PK-mineral fertiliser trial between 1990 and 2022, following the cessation of farmyard manure (FYM) fertilisation. To distinguish C-input from C-output pathways, we quantified the CO 2 exchange and the net ecosystem C balance (NECB) in 2022. During 33 years of NPK fertilisation, mean plant yield declined at a rate of 0.03 t C ha −1 yr −1 , and SOC stocks declined at 0.5 t C ha −1 yr −1 . In response to NPK treatments, plant yields showed a 1.6-fold difference, but the corresponding SOC stocks did not differ. In 2022, reference plots under continuous FYM fertilisation, still had the same SOC stocks as NPK plots in the zero year (1989). Short-term changes in SOC stock were not correlated with any climate indices or plant yields, but extreme weather conditions in 2022 coincided with abrupt changes in SOC stock. We conclude that in our experiment, grassland yield and long-term temperature increase were not modulators of SOC stock decline. Only under C and N import conditions, through FYM application, were historical SOC stocks maintained. In single years, hot and dry weather may cause large SOC stock losses of ca. 5 t C ha −1 yr −1 in the form of CO 2 .
Why Phytate Persists: Mechanisms of Exceptional Binding and Stability on Soil Mineral Surfaces Proxy
Environmental Science & Technology · 2026-11-03 · Aurore Niyitanga Manzi; Benjamin Barrios-Cerda; Christos D. Malliakas; Ludmilla Aristilde
Abstract Phytate, a six-phosphate compound that stores plant phosphorus, is a stable component of soil phosphorus, yet mechanisms underlying its persistence remain unclear. Here, we combined adsorption experiments, spectroscopy, X-ray scattering, and molecular modeling to investigate phytate interactions with five soil minerals: goethite, hematite, quartz, montmorillonite, and kaolinite. Iron oxides exhibited up to 5-fold greater adsorption capacities than quartz and clays. Natural organic matter caused negligible desorption of mineral-bound phytate, whereas phytate displaced adsorbed natural organic matter, demonstrating competitive and stable phytate-mineral associations. Adsorption capacities normalized to orthophosphate binding-site densities implied multiphosphate coordination for phytate on iron oxides, but binding beyond nominal orthophosphate sites on montmorillonite. Molecular dynamics simulations captured one to three phosphate groups in phytate associations with iron oxides; metal cation screening of electrostatic repulsion facilitated binding on negatively charged quartz and montmorillonite. Infrared spectroscopy revealed coupled vibrational responses of phosphate and inositol ring moieties on goethite, but primarily phosphate responses on montmorillonite. X-ray total scattering determined a feature at 2.7 Å, supporting inner-sphere coordination on iron oxides and consistent with quantum mechanics-based energy calculations, whereas quartz and clay features indicated interfacial reorganization. These findings establish mineral-specific mechanisms of phytate stabilization, with implications for persistence and bioavailability in soils.

Integrating reflective mulch with sufficient fertility sustains crop productivity while supporting sustainable pest management Proxy
Agriculture Ecosystems & Environment · 2026-11-02 · Arnol Gomez; Luis Canas; Matthew D. Kleinhenz; Manbir K. Rakkar; Ashley B Leach
Pest regulation and crop performance in agroecosystems emerge from interactions among agronomic management, plant physiological responses, and trophic dynamics, yet these drivers are often evaluated in isolation. In intensive vegetable systems, reliance on insecticide-based management can disrupt these ecological interactions without addressing the broader agroecosystem drivers of pest pressure. Western flower thrips exemplify this problem because their rapid population growth and feeding damage continue to challenge effective management. We conducted a two-year field study to evaluate how interacting agronomic and chemical factors shape pest suppression, predator responses, and crop yield. Using open-field peppers as a model system, a factorial experiment compared two insecticide programs (weekly spinetoram applications and untreated), three ground cover treatments (black mulch, diamond-reflective mulch, and bare soil), and three fertility rates (high NPK, sufficient NPK, and unfertilized). Predator responses were taxon- and sampling-method dependent, reflecting insecticide use and bottom-up agronomic context, likely mediated by prey availability and habitat modifications. Diamond-reflective mulch paired with sufficient/high NPK produced comparable marketable yields, whereas additional insecticide inputs did not improve fruit quality once pest densities fell below economic thresholds. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that environmental modification can reduce pest pressure and improve crop productivity, while nutrient management shapes important tradeoffs between crop performance and herbivore pressure, suggesting that environmental modification may serve as a scalable lever for sustainable pest regulation within integrated pest management programs.
Free-Radical Cascade Mediates Humus Formation in Mineral-Rich Organic Matrices Proxy
Environmental Science & Technology · 2026-11-02 · Kaipeng Zhai; Ying Lin; Keke Yin; Runxin Yang; Li Chen et al.
Abstract Humus formation in nature is traditionally viewed as a lengthy microbial enzymatic process, yet the abiotic pathways remain unclear. Here, by employing iron-mineral-rich composting as a model system that amplifies and accelerates natural humification, we reveal a free-radical cascade that drives abiotic humification. Magnetite addition amplified hydroxyl radical (•OH) production 1.5-fold (1289.4 μmol·kg–1) and enriched humus precursors (amino acids, polyphenols, reducing sugars), ultimately yielding 1.5 times more humic acid (135.6 mg/g) with higher molecular weight and stronger aromatic condensation. Mass spectrometry combined with density functional theory calculations identified multiple environmentally persistent free radicals (EPFRs), including semiquinone and phenoxy radicals as key intermediates that couple precursors into macromolecular humus. Simulated transformation experiments with selective microbial inactivation and •OH scavenging demonstrated that the abiotic radical pathway contributed approximately 33.4% to humus formation. We propose a free-radical cascade: •OH-driven oxidative fragmentation of organic matter followed by EPFR-mediated humus precursors coupling. These findings establish an unrecognized abiotic dimension of humification, reshaping our mechanistic understanding of organic carbon stabilization and offering nature-based strategies for soil carbon sequestration and remediation in organic-mineral interaction environments.
